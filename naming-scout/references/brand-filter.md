# Brand filter

Applied to every candidate before it earns a lookup. Cheap to run, and it removes most of
what makes AI naming output feel worthless.

## Fourteen tests

**1. Say it aloud.** Three times, at speaking pace, in a sentence: "We're using X for
that." Names that read well and speak badly are the most common failure in written-first
generation. Watch for consonant collisions at the seam of a compound (`stackscope`,
`craftfully`), stress that lands ambiguously, and anything that needs a run-up.

**2. The phone test.** Say it once, then spell it. If a listener would ask "how do you
spell that?", the name carries a permanent tax on every word-of-mouth referral. Some names
earn that tax. Most do not.

**3. Distinctiveness inside its own category.** Not "is it unique" but "does it sound like
its neighbours". Three companies in the same category using `-forge` means the fourth is
invisible. Check against the incumbents the user named.

**4. Memorability after one hearing.** Could the user's audience repeat it tomorrow?
Concrete beats abstract. Imageable beats conceptual. A name that draws a picture survives;
a name that describes a category does not.

**5. Length and shape.** Count syllables, not characters. Two or three syllables is the
comfortable range for spoken use. Look at the shape as a word. Ascenders and descenders make
a wordmark. A flat run of round letters does not.

Short is not automatically better, and the reason matters. Length helps memory and typing. It
does nothing for ranking, and short real words are the hardest names to own in search because
the word already had meanings before you did. Chase short and distinctive together. If you can
only have one, take distinctive. Full argument in `search-and-seo.md`.

Length becomes a hard limit only where a system imposes one: a CLI binary, an app store
display name, a social handle.

**6. Semantic fit.** Does the meaning point at what the thing does, or merely at a mood?
Mood-only names are legitimate for consumer brands with a marketing budget, and expensive
for anything that must explain itself in a search result.

**7. Positioning fit.** Does it support the wedge, or only the category? A name that would
suit any competitor equally well is a category name, not a brand name.

**8. Tone match.** Against the personality the user chose, and against the tone they said
would be wrong. A playful name for a compliance product is a mismatch even if it is a good
name.

**9. Headroom.** If the product doubles in scope, does the name still hold? Names that
encode a format (`weekly`, `podcast`, `newsletter`), a technology (`gpt`, `crypto`), a
number, or a single feature all lock the thing in place. Format lock is the most common
regret in newsletter and content-brand naming.

**10. Collision risk.** Does it sound like an existing brand, particularly one in an
adjacent category? Note it here; confirm it in clearance. Sounding like a bigger company is
a trademark exposure, not just an aesthetic problem.

**11. Explanation cost.** How much has to be said before the name makes sense? Zero is not
the goal: a name that needs one line of tagline can be stronger than a self-explanatory one.
Two lines is too many. If you cannot explain it in a sentence, neither can the user.

**12. Negative check.** Unintended readings when run together as a lowercase domain
(the `pen-island` class of error), unfortunate meanings in the languages the user named,
existing slang, and the initials.

**13. Searchability.** Grade the name `ownable`, `contested` or `crowded` by how hard it will
be to sit at the top of a search for your own name. A coined word is ownable on day one. A
common English word may never be yours. This is a cost on the same scale as an expensive
domain and it belongs in the report next to one. Method in `search-and-seo.md`.

**14. Spelling stability.** If the name comes from a non-Latin script, decide which
romanisation is the primary one and find out who holds the others. `dokan`, `dukan` and
`dukaan` are one word to your user and three strings to a registry. See
`naming-across-languages.md`.

## The one-sentence rule

Every candidate needs a sentence in this shape:

> **This works because [what the name means] connects to [what the product does] and lands
> as [the tone the user asked for].**

Concrete: "**Lodestar** works because a lodestar is what you navigate by when instruments
fail, which is exactly the job of a decision framework for founders, and the word is old
enough to sound established rather than trendy."

Not: "Lodestar is a clean, modern, memorable name with strong brand potential."

If you cannot write the first kind of sentence, the candidate is not ready. Cut it.

## The weakness rule

Every candidate also needs a stated weakness. Not a hedge. A specific, named cost.

> `-forge` is common in developer tooling; expect to share the category's sound.
> Two syllables read as a verb first, so the first hearing lands as an instruction.
> Requires a tagline to mean anything at all.
> The spelling is unambiguous only if the listener knows the word.

A candidate presented with no weakness has not been examined. If it genuinely has none,
say what it costs instead: an $8,000 domain, a crowded namespace, a coined word that will
need three years of marketing to mean something.

## Automatic cuts

- **Permutation artefacts.** `<concept>ly`, `<concept>AI`, `<concept>HQ`, `<concept>Labs`,
  `Get<concept>`, `<concept>ify`, dropped vowels (`Trakr`, `Flowr`). Exhausted, and they
  signal that no thought happened.
- **Near-duplicates of another candidate.** Two names from the same stem are one candidate
  with a variant, not two options. Pick the better one.
- **Descriptive to the point of being generic.** If it could be the category's Wikipedia
  article title, it cannot be owned, in trademark or in memory.
- **Novelty spelling of a common word.** `Kwik`, `Xpress`, `Fli`. Costs the phone test and
  buys nothing.
- **Anything only defensible by its domain being free.** This is the cut that matters most.

## Scoring, when the list is long

Rate 1–5 on memorability, distinctiveness, say-aloud, positioning fit, and headroom. Do not
average: a 1 on say-aloud or positioning fit is fatal regardless of the rest. Use scoring to
narrow, then rank by judgment. The composite number is a filter, not a verdict, and it should
never appear in the report.
