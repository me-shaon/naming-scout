# Search and SEO

How a name affects whether people can find you. This is a naming input, not a marketing
afterthought. Two shortlisted names can differ by years of work and a large budget in how
hard it is to rank for your own brand.

## Start by correcting the myth

Founders often believe a keyword domain ranks better. `bestcrmsoftware.com` beating
`pipedrive.com` for "best CRM software".

That stopped being true with Google's exact-match domain update in 2012. Keywords in a
domain are not a ranking signal now. Sites with keyword domains still appear because they
tend to be old, not because of the domain.

Say so plainly when a user pushes toward a descriptive name for SEO reasons. They are paying
a real brand cost for a benefit that no longer exists. Descriptive names have other merits.
Ranking is not one of them.

## The one thing that actually matters: can you own your own name

Every business gets searched by name. That query is your cheapest, highest-intent traffic.
The only question worth asking at naming time is how hard it will be to sit at the top of it.

Grade every serious candidate on one axis.

| Grade | What it looks like | What it costs you |
|---|---|---|
| **Ownable** | A coined word, an unusual compound, or a phrase nobody uses. `Pipedrive`, `Klarna`, `evenmonths`. | Nothing. You rank first within weeks of launch. Autocomplete learns the term and it belongs to you. |
| **Contested** | A real word with other established uses, or a name shared with companies in other categories. `Granary`, `Lodestar`, `Sidebar`. | Months to years of content and links. You will share page one with a dictionary entry, a Wikipedia article, or a bigger company. |
| **Crowded** | A common word or a phrase people already search for another reason. `Harvest`, `Basin`, `Mela`, `Buffer`. | You may never own it. Users adding your category to the query every time is a permanent tax on word of mouth. |

Put the grade in the report next to the domain state. It is the same kind of fact and it
costs more.

**How to grade it.** Search the bare name. Count how many results on page one are about
something else. Look for a Wikipedia entry, a dictionary definition, a large company, or a
place. Then search the name plus the category. If the bare name is crowded but name plus
category is clean, that is contested, not crowded. People will find you, but they will have
to type more.

## Short names and search

Users ask whether short is better. The honest answer is that length and searchability are
independent, and conflating them causes bad decisions.

**Length is not a ranking signal.** Google does not reward short domains. There is nothing
to optimise here.

**Short helps memory and typing.** Fewer syllables survive being heard once, repeated in
conversation, and typed on a phone. That is real value. It is about word of mouth rather
than about search.

**Short real words are the worst case for search.** `Basin`, `Mela`, `Haat` and `Granary` are
all short and all crowded. Shortness usually means the word already existed, which means it
already has meanings and results competing with you. A short generic word gives you the
memorability but not the ownership.

**Short coined words are the best case.** `Klarna`, `Zapier` and `Stripe` are short and
unique. Nothing else claims the term.

So the axis that matters is distinctiveness, not length. Chase short and distinctive
together. If you can only have one, take distinctive.

Two more things length actually decides:

- **Cost.** Every four and five letter `.com` is registered. Short means aftermarket.
- **Fit for the format.** A CLI binary, an app store display name (iOS truncates around 30
  characters) and a social handle all have real limits. Count syllables for speech, and
  characters only where a system imposes a limit.

## TLD and ranking

**No gTLD ranks better than another.** Google has said repeatedly that `.com`, `.io`, `.dev`,
`.xyz` and the rest are treated the same. There is no boost and no penalty. Choose on
credibility, price and availability.

**Some country TLDs are treated as generic.** Google publishes a list of country-code TLDs it
treats as generic rather than geographic, because they are used as words rather than for a
country. `.io`, `.co`, `.ai`, `.me`, `.tv` and `.fm` are the ones this skill sees most. Those
carry no geographic targeting.

**Every other ccTLD geo-targets you.** A `.de`, `.in` or `.com.bd` site is assumed to serve
that country and will struggle to rank elsewhere. That is exactly right for a local business
and wrong for anything global. This is the single largest SEO consequence of a TLD choice, so
raise it whenever a user is choosing between a local ccTLD and a global one.

Check the current generic list rather than trusting this file. Google moves TLDs on and off
it.

**Hyphens and numbers.** No ranking penalty. They cost you elsewhere. A hyphen has to be
spoken aloud every time and gets dropped when typed. A number raises "is that the digit or
the word". Both read as a signal that the real name was taken.

## Findability beyond the search engine

**Mishearing kills more traffic than ranking does.** If someone hears your name at a
conference and cannot spell it, they cannot search it. The phone test in `brand-filter.md` is
also a search test. A name that fails it loses the referral entirely, and no amount of SEO
recovers a search that never happened.

**Autocomplete and correction work against new brands.** Search engines will "correct" an
unusual name toward a common word for the first months. A coined name close to an existing
word inherits that word's corrections. Search your candidate and see what the engine
suggests instead.

**App store search is a separate channel.** For a mobile product, the store is where most
discovery happens and it works on keywords, not links. The app name field carries real
ranking weight there. A distinctive brand name plus a descriptive subtitle usually beats
either alone.

**Competitors can bid on your brand term.** Anyone can buy ads against your name. A
distinctive name makes those ads look wrong to the user. A generic name makes them look like
a normal result.

## What to report

For each serious candidate:

```
Searchability: ownable    Nothing else claims the term. First result within weeks.
Searchability: contested  Shares page one with a UK consultancy and a dictionary entry.
                          Expect to add the category to the query for the first year.
Searchability: crowded    "Harvest" returns a time-tracking product and the agricultural
                          sense. You would not own this term.
```

Never present a search result count as a finding. Absence of results is weak evidence, for
the reasons in `clearance-guide.md`. What you are reporting is competition for attention on a
term, not whether the term exists.
