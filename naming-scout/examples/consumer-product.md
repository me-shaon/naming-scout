# Example: consumer product

Shows no technical registry checks at all. Shows trademark as the dominant risk. Shows an
honest report when a territory does not recover.

*Checks run 2026-09-08. Re-run before deciding.*

---

**User:** Refillable, plastic-free dish soap. Aluminium bottle, refill tablets. The point is
that it looks good enough to leave on the counter instead of hiding under the sink. Premium
but warm, not clinical. Selling DTC in the US and UK.

**Scout:** This is a physical product sold commercially, so the risk profile differs from a
software name. Trademark matters more here than the domain. You can build a brand on a `.co`
or a two-word `.com`. You cannot build one on a name somebody else owns in class 3.

Checks: domain, trademark search in the US and UK for class 3, social handles at low
confidence, and a search pass for existing brands. No GitHub, no npm, no app stores.

Two questions:

1. Retail eventually, or DTC only? Retail means the name has to work on a shelf next to
   Method and Blueland, and it makes trademark a hard requirement.
2. Is a two-word `.com` acceptable, or does the brand need a single word?

**User:** Retail eventually, yes. Two words is fine if it's good.

## Territories

- **Water states.** Still, spring, rain, brine, tide. Warm and natural.
- **Domestic ritual.** Scullery, washhouse, basin, the daily rinse. Nostalgic and tactile.
  Fits "leave it on the counter".
- **Ceramics and glaze.** Delftware, saltglaze, glazeware. Unexpected for soap. It points at
  the object rather than the chemistry, which is the actual differentiator.
- **Plain speaking.** Plain soap, honest suds. Fits the anti-greenwashing angle. This is the
  most crowded language in the category.
- **Coined.** The route most successful brands here took. Blueland, Dropps, Cleancult. It
  costs marketing spend and buys a clean trademark.

Zero. Every warm domestic English word went in the 1990s. This is the hardest category in
the examples directory, and the reason is structural. Consumer brand naming has competed for
this vocabulary for thirty years, with money behind it.

## Round 2: ceramics, and less obvious water compounds

```

springhouse.com  registered 1995    rainhouse.com   registered 1999
quietwater.com   registered 1999    tidehouse.com   registered 2011
```

11% is a small recovery. It is not enough. Both survivors are weak. `softbasin` is a phrase
nobody says. `sudsmith` fails the phone test: is it "suds-smith" or "sud-smith"?

**This is where I stop pretending the round worked.** Two free domains attached to two
mediocre names is not a shortlist. The honest read of these two rounds is that the real-word
territory is closed on `.com` for this category. Your choice is between three routes, not
between these two names.

## The three real routes

**Route 1: buy from the aftermarket.** The best names in both rounds are listed, not free.
`glazeware.com` has been held since 1999 and sits on Afternic. `brinehouse.com` was
registered in 2025 and listed immediately, which makes it inventory rather than a business.
Neither price is visible from RDAP. For a retail-bound consumer brand this is usually the
right spend. The domain is a one-off cost against a brand you will spend far more on.

**Route 2: coin a word.** Blueland, Dropps, Cleancult and Cleanomic all did this. These two
rounds show why. Real words are gone. Coined words are trademark-clean. It costs you meaning,
which you then buy back with marketing.

**Route 3: a compound `.com` with a modifier.** `thescullery.com`, `sculleryco.com`. Cheap
and available. It also signals permanently that somebody else has the real name.

Take route 1 or route 2. Route 3 undercuts a premium positioning at the exact moment a
customer is deciding whether to trust the price.

## Candidates worth pursuing, by route

**Glazeware** (Ceramics, aftermarket)
The glaze is what makes a functional object worth looking at. That is the product thesis in
one word. Warm, tactile, pointed at the bottle rather than the chemistry.
*Weakness:* it reads as a homewares or ceramics brand before it reads as soap, so the
packaging and copy have to carry the category. "-ware" also suggests a product range, which
is a commitment if you only ever sell one thing.
`.com` for_sale on Afternic, held since 1999. Price not visible from RDAP.

**Brinehouse** (Water states, aftermarket)
Brine is salt water. Preserving, elemental, faintly coastal. "House" gives it a maker's
identity rather than a product name.
*Weakness:* brine also means pickling. For a product you put on things you eat off, the food
association does not help. It was registered in 2025 and listed at once, so expect
speculative pricing on a name with no history.
`.com` for_sale on Atom.

**Scullery** (Domestic ritual, approach the holder)
The best name in the run and the one I want. A scullery is the small back room where the
washing-up happened. That is exactly the humble task this brand is elevating, and the word is
warm, old and unmistakably domestic.
*Weakness:* it leans British. A US shopper may not know the word, which is a real cost when
half your market is US retail. `scullery.com` has been held since 2000 by a live registrant,
so this is an approach, not a purchase.
`.com` registered 2000 · worth checking `.co` and `scullerysoap.com`

**Softbasin and Sudsmith** are free and I do not recommend either. They are listed so you can
see what "available" produced in this category instead of taking my word for it.

## What I would do

Run one more round in the coined territory before committing. In parallel, get prices on
`glazeware.com` and `scullery.com`. If glazeware comes in under a few thousand it is the best
value in the run: a name with meaning, in a category where everyone else had to invent one.

## Check these yourself

- **No trademark search has been run.** For a retail-bound cleaning product this is the check
  that matters most. It has to happen before you spend anything on the domain. Class 3 is
  crowded, and consumer goods is where large companies actively police marks. Even when it is
  run, the strongest thing I can say is "no obvious conflict in the marks I searched". That is
  not clearance. Get an attorney search before packaging goes to print.
- Aftermarket prices are not in RDAP. `for_sale` means a listing exists. It says nothing about
  the price. Open the listings.
- Social handles were not checked. See `references/clearance-guide.md` for why not.
- The US and UK search passes covered the names alone. Neither covers non-indexed local
  brands, which are common in home goods.
